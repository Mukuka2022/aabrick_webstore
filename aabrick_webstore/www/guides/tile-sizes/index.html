A tile size is written as its two sides in millimetres. 600x600 is a tile 600mm on each side, which is 60cm, which covers just over a third of a square metre.

Bigger tiles, fewer joints

The main thing size changes is how many grout lines you end up looking at. A 600x1200 floor has roughly a quarter of the joints of a 300x600 one over the same area. Fewer joints reads as calmer and cleaner, and there is less grout to scrub.

The trade off is that a large tile needs a flatter floor. Any dip or hump under a 1200mm tile shows up as a rocking corner or a lipped edge, where a 300mm tile would have ridden over it. If the screed is uneven, either fix the floor or choose a smaller tile.

Does a big tile make a small room look bigger

Usually yes, because the eye counts joints rather than measuring walls. A small bathroom in 600x600 generally looks larger than the same room in 300x300. There is a limit: if a tile is so big that every one at the edge has to be cut in half, the cuts become the thing you notice.

A rough test before you commit: divide the room by the tile. If you get a whole number and a bit, fine. If you get a whole number and almost nothing, shift to the next size down and you will lose fewer tiles to the saw.

How many tiles are in a box

Boxes are sold by coverage rather than by count, and most of our ranges land at about 1.44 square metres a box:

Which means a 20 square metre room takes about fourteen boxes of most sizes before you add anything for cuts. Our tile calculator does this properly, including the wastage, and fills the box size in for you once you choose a tile.

Wall tile or floor tile

A floor tile can go on a wall. A wall tile should not go on a floor. Wall ranges have a lighter body that is not built to be walked on, and they will crack under furniture or a dropped tool. If you want the same look on both, buy the floor tile for both.
